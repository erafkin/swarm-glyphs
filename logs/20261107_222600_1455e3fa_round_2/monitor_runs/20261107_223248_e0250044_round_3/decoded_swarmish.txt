# 3. THE TASK

**High confidence, directly observed:** Eight agents exchanged structured messages while a public evaluator maintained stage and invalid-attempt counters. It began at stage 0, advanced to 1 after message 17, to 2 after message 80, and to 3 after message 108. There were 28 invalid attempts by the end. No explicit success text beyond stage 3 is supplied.

**Medium confidence:** The task required collaboration on several successive steps or submissions. Agents shared different local statements involving recurring entities, numerical-looking values, and nested propositions. They requested information from particular agents and cited each other. A long middle period involved repeated attempted steps accompanied by closely related messages. This could reflect solving the task, difficulty expressing commands in Swarmish, or both. The log does not establish whether task actions were directly executed by chat text or separately performed and then discussed.

**What they concretely said/did:**

- Initially they discussed numbered/described entities and recurring phrases N, L/F, and D. Several agents cited agent_5 concerning E(𘎌). Message 17 combined a correction/action-prefixed property statement with Y(I, `𗿃𘝚`), followed by stage 1.
- In the middle exchange they compared material P, Q, R, and S. Conditional-looking statements occur in 27, 30, and 34. They repeatedly used Z, X, and Y with those payloads. The stage-1 transition followed X(I,Q) together with Y(I,S), qualifier M. Closely related earlier exchanges had not advanced the stage.
- K repeatedly appears with numerical-looking values; P/R/S and Q are related to these values in 31–40. K could be a changing resource/state variable, but that English interpretation is only a guess.
- After the transition, exchanges using X(I,R) and Y(I,S) were followed by failures. The agents returned to information exchange about earlier entities and values. They cited agent_0 and agent_3 particularly in 103–106.
- The final message asserted H(F, a descriptive phrase ending in num(𘎌)) and Y(agent_0,G). Stage 3 followed without another invalid attempt.

**Low-confidence task hypotheses:** A distributed-clue/epistemic puzzle, a stateful protocol/command puzzle, or a planning/resource-precondition puzzle all fit parts of the evidence. Numbered E entities could be clue slots, statements, or other task entities. P/R/S could be propositions, short action descriptions sharing an initial verb, or literal submission content. We cannot securely identify a familiar named puzzle, exact objective, exact values, or the English content of the final answer. We do not infer doors, keys, passwords, particular resources, or exact digits without an anchor.

**Conclusion actually supported:** The agents reached all three visible stage transitions. Their final substantive content includes G and an H relation between F and a phrase ending in num(𘎌). The English meaning of that conclusion remains unknown. Success feedback establishes progression, not that our lexical hypotheses—or all the agents' intermediate statements—are correct.

# 4. EVIDENCE

## Strong structural evidence

1. **Balanced clauses and nesting.** The same opening and closing tokens enclose simple reports and nested propositions, especially in 7–9, 19, 21, 30, and 34. Doubled closing tokens at the ends of embedded reports support recursive syntax. In 7, the marked H predicate follows the embedded clause but remains inside the outer clause.
2. **Role markers rather than fixed order.** Message 2 places its marked subject before the predicate; many messages put predicate first. Messages 28 and 59 show further argument-order variation. This supports labeled slots rather than a purely positional word-for-word substitution. It predicts that moving a marked span preserves its role, whereas changing its marker changes the role.
3. **Alternate-register parallel.** The final passage of 52 has predicate-marker/V, subject-marker/Q, complement-marker/R, close. This matches the ordinary V(Q,R) clause in 41/42. Message 10 corroborates the same proposed role markers in a different order. This is structural evidence independent of task success.
4. **Attribution patterns.** `𘅌𘋟` repeatedly precedes agent_5 in early confirmation/report-like clauses and agent_0/agent_3 in late ones. Source/evidence is a better provisional interpretation than an ordinary lexical conjunction.
5. **Self/name substitutions.** The structures in 72/76/78 and 86/92 replace I with the speaker's explicit identifier while retaining closely matching predicates and payloads. This supports self-reference, though singular “I” versus collective “we” is unresolved.
6. **Numeric family.** Tokens beginning `𘔠` occupy repeated value positions and combine with a stable preceding token in E(g). Distinct suffixes recur across reports. This supports numeric typing, not particular digit values or their order.
7. **Dictionary-family regularity.** Most vocabulary is two glyphs with a small first-glyph inventory. Distributional classes are apparent, but predicate uses of B and IF? show that strict noun/verb/adjective assignments would overstate the evidence.

## Useful contrasts, not English lexical ground truth

- Messages 72 and 78 are identical; 80 changes the second payload R to S and precedes a stage advance. The first clause remains X(I,Q), and M remains present.
- Messages 84 and 86 differ in the presence of M; both precede failures in stage 2. M alone cannot explain success.
- Messages 41 and 42 are identical, but feedback appears only after the latter. Message 25 resembles an attributed information report yet is followed by an invalid-attempt update. Thus feedback may track private/separate actions rather than directly interpreting the preceding chat clause. We cannot confidently translate Y as an executable “submit” command solely from adjacency.
- The same H/B relations and long nominal-like phrases recur before and after the protocol-attempt sequence, suggesting continued discussion of shared task data.
- The unary proposition `𗌿𗩠`(subject `𗡒𗎿`) in 101 also occurs embedded in 30 and as part of S. This makes a compositional proposition/action interpretation plausible; treating S as an atomic password is not required.
- P/S are related by W to numerical K phrases in 31/37, while other predicates relate R/Q to numerical K phrases. This motivates the low-confidence resource/precondition alternative, without decoding any action or resource name.

## Limits and what would change the reconstruction

The largest missing piece is the lexical codebook. Most translations are honest partial parses, not recovered English. An independent anchor—a word paired with an unambiguous referent, explicit arithmetic, a clear paraphrase, or a spelled-out command—could substantially improve them. An actual number mapping might reveal a recognizable puzzle. A known translation of H, B, P, Q, R, S, K, or G could change the task hypothesis completely.

Evidence that the clause-prefix operators are mandatory speech-act tags would revise the tentative request/confirmation/correction labels. Evidence that the argument markers are typed fields rather than grammatical subject/object would change those names while retaining the observed slot structure. An actual productive spelling rule, longer lexical forms, or a cipher key could replace the dictionary-family hypothesis. If the alternate passage in 52 were shown not to express V(Q,R), the proposed alternate-register aliases would weaken substantially.

We deliberately have not inferred English words from visual appearance or Unicode identities, assigned digits from frequency/appearance order, assumed intermediate claims were true, or treated task progression as translation ground truth.
