1. SWARMISH.TXT, RECONSTRUCTED

This is a partial reconstruction, not a recovered dictionary. Confidence labels concern decoding, not merely whether a glyph appears. The log supports a small number of probable numerical meanings and recurring conversational frames. Exact words and grammar remain substantially uncertain.

Observed writing conventions
• [HIGH] Messages use space-separated Tangut-looking glyphs, generally one glyph per apparent word/token. No punctuation or decimal numerals occur in the agents' messages.
• [HIGH] Several short sequences recur verbatim; messages often concatenate two such sequences without a visible sentence separator.
• [MEDIUM] The agents appear to use a report frame and an inquiry/request frame. The inferred functions are supported by alternation and conversational distribution, but their literal wording is unknown.
• [LOW] Word order may be flexible or telegraphic. Alternatively, some messages are malformed experiments. The log does not establish that every observed arrangement is grammatical Swarmish.
• [HIGH] No discarded-message marker ⊘ appears in this log. We cannot reconstruct the full permitted alphabet from this sample.

3. THE TASK

The supplied task is a cooperative escape-room puzzle, Storm Shelter. The agents start with distributed clues for a four-digit cabinet code. Cabinet success would release power-panel clues; successful BOOT would release exit clues. This task description comes from the briefing, not from a complete decoding of the chat.

The visible conversation is consistent with the cabinet phase. Agent_0 repeatedly reports a payload plausibly meaning 7; agent_2 similarly reports 8. Agent_4 and agent_5 use the probable numeral 2, agent_6 and agent_7 use probable 4, agent_1 uses probable 3, and agent_3 uses probable 1. These match their numerical cabinet clues, including the distinction that some agents know a position index while others know a base digit. The chat does not clearly preserve that distinction in a decodable way, nor does it establish a symbol-name vocabulary.

After these early reports, the conversation becomes dominated by repeated inquiry-like formulas. There is no recoverable collective conclusion assigning the four symbol positions to the four base digits, no clearly assembled four-digit cabinet code, and no recognizable transition to discussing power costs, prerequisites, or exit offsets.

The only public simulator status is [1], stage 0 with zero invalid attempts. Consequently, the strongest safe conclusion is that the log shows no demonstrated progress beyond the cabinet and appears to show stalled communication. We cannot conclude that nobody tried an action: action submissions and results were private. Nor can we derive a final invalid-attempt count from the initial status alone.

For comparison only, the briefing's full solution is cabinet 2748; power sequence DRAIN → CRANK → SIGNAL → BOOT, with battery 4 → 2 → 5 → 2 → 0; exit 9796. Those are supplied facts, NOT recovered agent conclusions. Nothing in the chat justifies translating a message as “the cabinet code is 2748” or “the exit code is 9796.”

Does the log fit the task? Yes, partially: the source-linked probable numerals fit the distributed opening clues particularly well, and the repetitive questions fit a coordination failure. It does not exhibit the successful worked solution, and its sparse recoverable vocabulary cannot independently establish every task detail.

4. EVIDENCE

A. Numerical anchors and their limits
The strongest evidence is contrastive rather than a single convenient clue match. Agent_0 repeatedly uses 𗋭 in a stable report position; agent_2 repeatedly uses 𗸪 in that position. Their private cabinet digits are 7 and 8. More importantly, 𗳃 is shared by agents whose different clues both contain 2, and 𘙠 by agents whose different clues both contain 4. That overlap makes a numeral reading more plausible than four separate symbol-name readings. Agent_1's 𗸽 and agent_3's 𗛽 extend the hypothesis to 3 and 1.

This remains medium confidence because known task facts are doing substantial anchoring. Agent_3 initially uses a different payload, 𗶞, and the log does not provide arithmetic or an independently recognizable digit sequence that verifies the mapping. Repeated agreement among decoders is not additional evidence.

B. Parallel clause structure
Compare [31] and [43]: the same probable-7 report changes 𗟎 to 𗡒. Similar alternation occurs with probable 4 and 8. This supports related grammatical roles but does not identify either word.

Compare [49], [79], and especially [87]: a clause starting 𗏔 is followed by one starting 𗵵 with otherwise similar scaffolding. This favors I/my versus you/your, but alternatives involving report versus request verbs remain possible. The common prefixed 𗄪 therefore looks like an inquiry introducer; exact wording cannot be recovered.

C. Why two grammatical analyses are retained
The common report can be parsed approximately as “I / have / number / 7,” but also as “my / number / is / 7.” Messages [19,29,57,98] put 𗿃 after a probable numeral, and [12,54,74] omit it. These variations prevent a secure one-to-one assignment of have, number, and is. Repetitions of 𗿃 in [67,109] further caution against treating every message as a clean grammatical exemplar.

D. Recurring inquiries, not confidently decoded requests for a particular code
The long formula 𗄪 𗵵 𗟎 𗿃 𗣑 𗎰 recurs across many senders, especially late in the log. Its placement after numerical reports and its cross-agent convergence make a request/question function plausible. But we cannot distinguish “what is your number?” from “tell me all your information,” “what else do you know?” or similar meanings. Accordingly, 𗣑 is not confidently assigned all, code, what, or missing; 𗎰 is not confidently assigned please or a question mark.

E. Rare glyphs and failures of fluent translation
Unknown tokens are explicitly preserved in the translation. No unused numeral slots have been filled merely to complete a decimal alphabet. In particular, 𗦴 has no consistent clue-linked numerical behavior. Likewise 𗶞, 𗚀, 𘉝, and the one-off tokens cannot be identified by their positions alone. The unusual messages may be grammatical constructions outside the recovered frames, or may be agents' unsuccessful attempts to improvise.

F. What would change this reconstruction
An actual dictionary, agents' contemporaneous explanations, or additional conversations with independent plaintext anchors could overturn any tentative lexical assignment. Evidence that 𗋭/𗸪 refer to MOON/WAVE rather than 7/8 would require revising the numerical map; a second context separating symbol names from values would be especially diagnostic. Repeated examples with known questions, negation, or explicit multi-digit codes could distinguish the competing grammatical analyses and identify suffixes. Additional public stage feedback or private action records could revise the apparent-stalling conclusion without necessarily changing the linguistic analysis.

The present reconstruction is deliberately incomplete. It identifies probable numerical content and interaction patterns, not a fully broken language.
